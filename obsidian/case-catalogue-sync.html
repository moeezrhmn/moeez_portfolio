Case 02 · Commerce, Automation

THREE HUNDRED THOUSAND LISTINGS, ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH.

A UK retailer was maintaining the same catalogue by hand in two storefronts. This is what replaced that, and what it cost them not to have it sooner.

The brief

The client sold the same catalogue through a Shopify storefront and an eBay shop. Both were maintained by hand. When a price changed, someone edited it twice. When stock moved, someone remembered to update the other channel, or did not.

The cost of forgetting was not theoretical. Overselling on eBay meant cancelling orders, which meant refunds, seller rating damage and support time. The team's answer had been to keep a buffer of unsold stock on eBay, which quietly capped how much they could sell.

One person spent roughly a full working day each week on reconciliation. The brief I was given was narrow: make the two catalogues agree, and stop the overselling. I later led the small team that built and extended it.

What made it
hard

The naive version of this, a script that loops every product and pushes it, fails on all four of these.

01

Rate limits

Both APIs cap calls per second. A full catalogue pass at 300k items is days of API budget, not minutes.

02

Different data models

Shopify variants and eBay listings do not map one to one. Categories, attributes and shipping profiles all needed translation.

03

Partial failure

A sync that dies halfway must not leave the two systems in a worse state than it found them.

04

No downtime

The shop kept trading throughout. Nothing could be rebuilt from scratch behind a maintenance page.

The system, as built

Fig. 1 · data flow

Shopify is the single source of truth. Everything downstream is derived, which means recovery is always "replay from Shopify" rather than "reconcile two opinions".

Five decisions that
made it work

A nightly full catalogue sweep would have burned the entire API budget and still left up to 24 hours of drift. Instead the engine subscribes to Shopify webhooks and only ever touches what actually changed.

A reconciliation sweep still runs, but weekly and in small batches, purely as a safety net for webhooks that never arrived. Typical sync latency went from a day to under a minute.

Webhooks arrive more than once. Retries replay work. If applying the same update twice produced a different result, the catalogue would slowly corrupt itself.

Each job carries the target state rather than an instruction to change something, so running it once and running it five times land in exactly the same place. That is what makes aggressive retrying safe.

Some updates genuinely cannot be applied: a category eBay rejects, an attribute that fails validation. Silently dropping those is how sync systems lose trust.

After three attempts with backoff, a job moves to a dead letter queue with its payload and the API's actual error message. Someone reviews a short list each morning instead of auditing 300,000 products. That list is the only manual work left in the system.

The shop was trading the whole time, so nothing was cut over at once. The engine ran in read only mode first, logging what it would have changed, and we compared that against reality for a week.

Then writes were enabled for a single low risk category, then a few more, then the rest. By the time it owned the full catalogue, it had already been proven correct on a real subset of it, and the team trusted it, which mattered as much as the code did.

The first version was Laravel, which shipped fast and proved the model. As the system grew, the data handling and concurrency work fit Python better than PHP did, and the team's centre of gravity was moving that way too.

So we moved roughly 80% of the system to Python a slice at a time, keeping both running against the same database while each piece was cut across. Nothing was rewritten wholesale and the shop never stopped trading. I led that migration and reviewed the work as the junior developers on the team took pieces of it.

What I'd do
differently

Build the exception dashboard first. For the first few weeks the dead letter queue was inspected through database queries, which meant only I could triage it. A simple screen would have handed that to the ops team a month earlier.

Instrument sooner. Metrics on queue depth and sync lag were added after the first incident rather than before it. They should have shipped with the first write enabled category. They are cheap up front and expensive to retrofit under pressure.

Push harder on the attribute mapping. A chunk of early dead letter volume came from category attributes that were guessable but not agreed. A day with the client's product lead at the start would have removed weeks of noise.
